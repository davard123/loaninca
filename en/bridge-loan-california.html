Bridge Loans in California: How to Buy a New Home Before You Sell Your Current One

A residential bridge loan is short-term financing secured by your current home that lets you use its equity for the next down payment before it sells. Fannie Mae accepts a bridge loan as a down payment source if it is not cross-collateralized against the new home and the lender documents that you can carry both homes plus the bridge loan (B3-4.3-14). Until your current home is under a signed sales contract with the buyer's financing contingencies cleared, its payment and the bridge loan payment generally count in your DTI (B3-6-06, B3-6-05).

How a residential bridge loan works

A bridge lender places a loan on the home you already own, and the proceeds go toward the down payment and costs on the new home. When the old home sells, the sale proceeds pay off the bridge loan. Terms such as maximum loan amount, how payments work, fees and the term length are set by each lender and are not covered by a single public standard, so this page does not list them. Terms vary by lender; ask for a written quote.

How your current home counts in DTI

  • Not sold, or only listed: if the sale will not close before the new purchase, the lender uses both the current home's PITIA and the new home's PITIA (B3-6-06).
  • Under contract with contingencies cleared: Fannie Mae does not require the current PITIA if the lender gets the executed sales contract and confirmation that any financing contingencies have been cleared.
  • Keeping it as a rental: under B3-3.8-05, the lender takes 75% of market rent (from an appraisal, Form 1007 or at least three comparable rentals; a lease is not accepted for a departing residence) and subtracts the home's PITIA. A positive result only offsets that PITIA; a negative result counts in DTI. If you have less than 12 months of property management experience, the lender must also verify six months of PITIA reserves for that home.

How the bridge loan counts

Under B3-6-05, a bridge loan is a contingent liability that must be counted in your monthly debts. The lender can exclude it on the same two documents: a fully executed sales contract for the current home and confirmation that financing contingencies have been cleared. Fannie Mae's DU allows a DTI up to 50% (B3-6-02); jumbo lenders set their own limits.

Alternatives to a bridge loan

  • HELOC on your current home: money borrowed against your real estate is an acceptable down payment source, and its payment counts as a debt (B3-4.3-15; B3-6-05). Ask before you list whether a lender will open a HELOC, or let you keep drawing on one, once the home is on the market. HELOCs usually have variable rates (CFPB).
  • Sell first, or close both on the same day: if the down payment comes from the sale, the lender needs the settlement statement from the old home at or before the new closing (B3-4.3-10).
  • Contingent offer: make the purchase contingent on selling your home. It avoids carrying two homes, but sellers in competitive markets may prefer non-contingent offers. Your agent can explain how the contingency is written.
  • Rent back: sell first and negotiate to stay in the home for a period after closing. Terms are set in the purchase contract.

What to ask before you choose

  • What is the total cost of the bridge loan or HELOC, including fees, and how long until funds are available?
  • Can you qualify with both payments counted if the old home takes longer to sell?
  • How many months could you carry both homes from savings?
  • Is the new loan conforming or jumbo, and does that lender accept bridge or HELOC funds for the down payment?

Worked example

This example uses made-up numbers to show how the DTI rules work. It is not a real client and not an approval.

A couple earns $25,000 a month before taxes. Their current home's PITIA is $4,000, the new home's PITIA will be $8,500, they have a $500 car payment, and they plan a bridge loan with an assumed $1,000 monthly payment (an assumption, not a quote).

  • Old home only listed: ($4,000 + $8,500 + $500 + $1,000) ÷ $25,000 = 56%, over DU's 50% maximum.
  • Old home under contract, buyer's financing contingency cleared: the old PITIA and the bridge payment can both be excluded: ($8,500 + $500) ÷ $25,000 = 36%.

Timing matters as much as the loan. Getting the old home under contract, with contingencies removed, before the new loan is underwritten can change the result.

Frequently asked questions

How does a bridge loan work for buying a home?

A bridge loan borrows against the equity in the home you own now so you can make the down payment on the new home before the old one sells. The bridge loan is paid off from the sale proceeds.

Does my current mortgage count in DTI when I buy before selling?

Yes, under Fannie Mae rules, unless the lender has the executed sales contract for your current home and confirmation that the buyer's financing contingencies have been cleared.

Does the bridge loan payment count in DTI?

Generally yes. Fannie Mae treats it as a contingent liability. It can be excluded with the same two documents: a fully executed sales contract and confirmation that financing contingencies are cleared.

Is a HELOC better than a bridge loan?

It depends on cost, timing and whether you can still open one. A HELOC payment also counts in DTI, some lenders will not open one on a home that is already listed, and HELOC rates are usually variable. Compare written terms.

Can I just say I will rent out my old home?

Only if that is your real plan. The lender then uses 75% of market rent minus the home's payment, and may require six months of reserves for it. If your plans change, tell your lender.
