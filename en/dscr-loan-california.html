DSCR Loans in California: How Rental Income Qualifies an Investment Property

A DSCR (debt service coverage ratio) loan qualifies a rental property mainly on whether its rent covers its payment: DSCR = monthly qualifying rent ÷ monthly PITIA (principal, interest, taxes, insurance and HOA dues). A ratio of 1.00 means the rent exactly covers the payment. DSCR loans are non-QM products, not Fannie Mae loans, so the minimum ratio, down payment, credit score and other terms are set by each lender; federal Truth in Lending rules generally treat credit to buy a non-owner-occupied rental as business-purpose credit (Regulation Z comment 3(a)-4).

Who uses DSCR loans

  • Investors whose tax returns show low income after depreciation and write-offs.
  • Self-employed borrowers who would rather not qualify on personal income.
  • Investors who own several rental properties and want each one to qualify on its own cash flow.
  • Buyers who want to hold title in an LLC; many DSCR programs allow it, but check each program.

DSCR loans are for investment property. They are not for a home you will live in.

The formula, piece by piece

The top number: qualifying rent. On a purchase, lenders typically use the market rent from the appraiser's rent schedule (Fannie Mae's Form 1007 is the common one) and may compare it to a signed lease. Which figure counts, and how short-term rental income is treated, depends on the program. Your own estimate of future rent does not count.

The bottom number: PITIA. Principal and interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance and HOA dues. In California, include any Mello-Roos special tax too, because it is billed with property taxes. Using principal and interest alone makes the ratio look better than it is.

How DSCR loans differ from a conventional investment loan

A conventional investment property loan follows Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac rules: your personal income, a DTI ratio and the agency's rental-income method (see Fannie Mae B3-3.8-01). A DSCR loan replaces most of that with the property's cash flow, but you still go through credit, asset, appraisal and title review, and the lender may ask for entity documents if you borrow through an LLC. Because each lender writes its own guidelines, two DSCR offers can look very different. This page does not list minimum ratios, credit scores, down payments or rates, because those are lender-specific and change.

What to compare between DSCR offers

  • How the lender calculates the ratio: which rent, and which expenses in PITIA.
  • Minimum ratio for your scenario and how pricing changes above and below it.
  • Prepayment penalty: length and structure. This matters if you might sell or refinance in a few years.
  • Points, lender fees, appraisal and rent-schedule costs, and reserve requirements.
  • Whether the program accepts LLC vesting, short-term rentals, condos or cash-out.

Ask each lender for a written Loan Estimate or written terms so you can compare line by line (CFPB guide to comparing Loan Estimates).

Why the ratio moves

Every input can change between your first estimate and closing: the rate on your final quote, the insurance premium, a reassessed property tax figure, a higher HOA due, or an appraiser's rent that comes in below your expectation. Recalculate the ratio each time one of them changes instead of relying on the first number. If the ratio falls short, the usual levers are a larger down payment, a different property, or a program that accepts a lower ratio at a different price.

What to prepare

  • Property address, price, expected rent and any existing lease.
  • Tax, insurance, HOA and Mello-Roos amounts.
  • Bank or brokerage statements for down payment and reserves.
  • LLC formation documents and operating agreement, if you are buying in an entity.

Worked example

This example uses made-up numbers. It is not a real client, a quote or a minimum requirement. Rate: 7.0% on a 30-year fixed loan, assumed for illustration only; not a quote and not a current market rate.

An investor buys an $800,000 single-family rental with a $600,000 loan. The appraiser's market rent is $5,000 a month.

  • Principal and interest: $600,000 × $6.653 per $1,000 ≈ $3,992.
  • Property tax (assumed 1.1% a year): $800,000 × 1.1% ÷ 12 ≈ $733.
  • Insurance (assumed) $150; HOA $300.
  • PITIA ≈ $3,992 + $733 + $150 + $300 = $5,175.

DSCR = $5,000 ÷ $5,175 ≈ 0.97. To reach 1.00 at the same rent, principal and interest would have to fall to $5,000 − $1,183 = $3,817, which at the same assumed rate means a loan of about $573,700, roughly $26,300 more down payment. Whether 0.97 is acceptable, and at what price, is up to the lender.

Frequently asked questions

What is a DSCR loan and how does it work?

It is an investment property loan that qualifies mainly on the property's rent compared with its monthly payment (principal, interest, taxes, insurance and HOA), instead of on your personal income and DTI.

What DSCR ratio do I need?

There is no single standard. DSCR loans are lender-specific non-QM products, and each lender sets its own minimum ratio and pricing. Ask for the lender's guideline in writing.

Can I get a DSCR loan in an LLC?

Many DSCR programs allow LLC borrowers, but the documents and personal guarantee requirements vary by lender. Confirm before you make an offer.

Can I use a DSCR loan for a home I will live in?

No. DSCR loans are for investment properties. A home you will occupy goes through regular owner-occupied loan rules.

Does a DSCR loan need tax returns?

Usually the property's rent replaces personal income documentation, but lenders still review credit, assets, the appraisal and, for an LLC, entity documents. The exact list depends on the program.
