Why Did I Get a Supplemental Tax Bill in California? (And Does Escrow Pay It?)

A supplemental tax bill is an extra property tax bill the county issues after a change in ownership or completed new construction, covering the difference between the new assessed value and the old one for the rest of the fiscal year (Revenue and Taxation Code §§75–75.72; California BOE). Lenders do not receive supplemental bills, even when they pay your regular taxes from an impound account, so the bill goes to you. The OC Treasurer-Tax Collector says most lenders do not pay them; call your loan servicer to confirm.

Why new owners get one

California property is assessed at its value when it changes hands or is newly built. Your regular annual tax bill for the current fiscal year (July 1 to June 30) was based on the previous owner's assessed value. When you buy, the assessor reappraises the property as of the date of the change, and the county bills the difference for the months left in the fiscal year. This is in addition to the regular bill, not a replacement for it.

How the amount is calculated

  • Net supplemental value = new assessed value − prior assessed value.
  • Multiply by the tax rate for the property.
  • Prorate by the months remaining in the fiscal year. The change takes effect on the first day of the month after the purchase or completion. The BOE's examples: a January 1 effective date uses a factor of 0.50 (6/12); a December 1 date uses 0.58 (7/12).

One bill or two

  • Change of ownership or completion from June 1 through December 31: one supplemental bill, for the rest of the current fiscal year.
  • From January 1 through May 31: two bills, one for the rest of the current fiscal year and one for the full next fiscal year starting July 1. The regular bill for that next year was already set on the old value, which is why the second supplemental bill exists.
  • If the new value is lower than the old one, you get a refund instead.

Why escrow and your impound account usually do not cover it

At closing, escrow prorates the regular tax bill between you and the seller. The supplemental bill does not exist yet. Later, the county mails it directly to the owner. The BOE says lenders do not receive these bills even if they pay the annual bill through an impound account, and that penalties cannot be excused because of a misunderstanding with a lender. Orange County's Treasurer-Tax Collector puts it plainly: most lenders do not pay supplemental bills, so contact your lender to find out.

When it arrives and when it is due

There is no fixed date. The assessor first mails a notice of supplemental assessment, then the tax collector mails the bill. Orange County says supplemental bills are mailed three to four times a year (important dates). Under Revenue and Taxation Code §75.52, a supplemental bill is payable in two installments. For a bill mailed July through October, the installments go delinquent on December 10 and April 10. For a bill mailed November through June, the first installment goes delinquent on the last day of the month after the bill is mailed, and the second on the last day of the fourth month after that. A 10% penalty applies to late installments. The due dates are printed on the bill; go by the bill.

What to do

  • Set aside money in your first year of ownership. A rough estimate is (price − seller's assessed value) × tax rate × months remaining ÷ 12.
  • When the bill arrives, call your servicer (the company you send payments to) and ask whether it will pay it. If not, pay the county yourself before the delinquency date.
  • Expect your monthly payment to change later: future regular bills use the new value, and the servicer's annual escrow analysis will catch up.
  • If you think the new assessed value is wrong, the notice explains how and when to appeal. Talk to the county assessor.

Worked example

This example uses made-up numbers to show the math. It is not a real client and not a tax estimate for any property.

A buyer closes on October 10. The price is $1,000,000 and the seller's prior assessed value was $400,000. Assume a combined tax rate of 1.1% (rates vary by area; this is an assumption).

  • Net supplemental value: $1,000,000 − $400,000 = $600,000.
  • Full-year tax on the difference: $600,000 × 1.1% = $6,600.
  • The change takes effect November 1, leaving 8 months (November through June) in the fiscal year: $6,600 × 8 ÷ 12 = $4,400.

Because the purchase fell between June 1 and December 31, there is one supplemental bill. Had the buyer closed on February 10 instead, there would be two: about $6,600 × 4 ÷ 12 = $2,200 for March through June, and about $6,600 for the next full fiscal year.

Frequently asked questions

Why did I get a supplemental tax bill?

Because the property changed ownership or new construction was completed. The county reassessed it and billed the difference between the new and old assessed values for the rest of the fiscal year.

Does my escrow or impound account pay the supplemental tax bill?

Usually not. The bill goes to the owner, not the lender. Orange County says most lenders do not pay these bills. Call your servicer to confirm, and pay it yourself if they will not.

Why did I get two supplemental tax bills?

If the purchase or completion happened between January 1 and May 31, the county issues two bills: one for the rest of the current fiscal year and one for the full next fiscal year.

Wasn't the property tax already settled at closing?

Closing prorates the regular tax bill between buyer and seller. The supplemental bill comes later, after the reassessment, so it was not part of the closing statement.

Do I get a supplemental bill on a new construction home?

Yes. Completed new construction is one of the events that triggers a supplemental assessment, according to the California BOE.
